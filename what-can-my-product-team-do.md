# What Can My Product Team Do?

This skills repository is designed to operate as more than a collection of specialist AI roles. Used individually or in combination, the skills can support work across the full product lifecycle: from investigating an early idea, through research, strategy, design and delivery, to launch, measurement and iteration.

The most useful way to think about the repository is therefore not **"which skills are available?"**, but **"what jobs can my product team undertake?"**

At its broadest, the team can help move work through the full product cycle, which this repository treats as a **triple diamond**:

> **Discover → Define → Develop → Decide → Build → Deliver and learn**

Three diamonds — problem space, solution space, delivery space — each widening the options before narrowing them, and each closed by a gate that opens both ways: **Problem agreed**, **Approach agreed**, **Outcome evidenced**.

The capabilities below describe what can be achieved by orchestrating the appropriate skills around a product problem. For which capability belongs at which phase, what the repeated ones do differently at each, and what each phase writes to the PPoT, see the **[capability map](./docs/capability-map.md)**.

## Preserve project knowledge

Use the Project Point of Truth practice to keep durable product context available beyond one conversation or delivery cycle.

The team can:

- Brief a new contributor from the project’s confirmed facts and active decisions.
- Answer product questions without presenting assumptions as facts.
- Record proposed facts, decisions, constraints, assumptions, risks, questions, and learning.
- Check a PRD, roadmap, design, implementation, or plan against established project knowledge.
- Identify contradictions between the PPoT and another credible source.
- Review stale, unsupported, duplicated, or ownerless knowledge.
- Surface durable learning produced by any other product-team practice.
- Preserve human approval before project knowledge is confirmed or published.

Every skill uses the same pull/push handshake: read relevant `PPoT.md` context before substantive work, then offer any durable new knowledge back as a proposal. See [Using the Project Point of Truth](./docs/PPOT.md).

## Turn an idea into a product

Start with a relatively undeveloped concept and use the team to progressively turn it into something viable, testable and buildable.

The team can:

- Interrogate a raw idea and determine whether there is a meaningful problem behind it.
- Identify likely users, customers and stakeholders.
- Separate known facts from assumptions.
- Research the market and existing alternatives.
- Assess differentiation and competitive opportunity.
- Establish a product vision and value proposition.
- Define strategic boundaries and product principles.
- Identify the riskiest assumptions.
- Determine what should be validated before committing to delivery.
- Define an MVP.
- Establish measurable outcomes and success criteria.
- Produce product requirements.
- Design the experience.
- Determine an appropriate technical architecture.
- Break the product into implementable work.
- Support implementation.
- Verify the result.
- Prepare the product for release.
- Establish how performance should be measured afterwards.

This allows a relatively simple request such as **"I have an idea for a product"** to become a structured product-development process rather than a single AI response.

## Run product discovery

Use the team to investigate an opportunity before deciding what should be built.

It can:

- Define a discovery problem.
- Identify what is currently known and unknown.
- Separate evidence from assumptions.
- Establish research questions.
- Create a discovery plan.
- Design customer interviews.
- Create interview scripts.
- Analyse interview transcripts.
- Cluster qualitative evidence.
- Identify recurring behaviours and pain points.
- Identify unmet needs.
- Identify jobs-to-be-done.
- Examine customer-support evidence.
- Compare qualitative findings with behavioural data.
- Identify contradictory evidence.
- Generate hypotheses.
- Identify the assumptions carrying the greatest risk.
- Design experiments to test those assumptions.
- Define validation criteria.
- Recommend whether to pursue, investigate further, pivot or stop.

By combining research, data, product, customer and adversarial perspectives, discovery can be based on **evidence triangulation** rather than a single source of truth.

## Investigate a product question

Treat an ambiguous product problem as an investigation rather than jumping immediately to solutions.

For example:

> **Why has activation fallen?**

The team can investigate:

- Funnel performance.
- Behavioural changes.
- UX friction.
- Content and comprehension problems.
- Accessibility barriers.
- Technical failures.
- Customer feedback.
- Acquisition mix.
- Onboarding.
- Pricing and paywall effects.
- Recent product changes.
- Correlations between releases and behavioural changes.
- Alternative explanations.
- Gaps in the available evidence.

The output can then be synthesised into:

- Probable causes.
- Supporting evidence.
- Contradictory evidence.
- Confidence levels.
- Unknowns.
- Recommended next investigations.
- Candidate interventions.

## Make product decisions

Use multiple specialist perspectives to improve important product decisions.

The team can evaluate competing options against factors such as:

- Customer value.
- Business value.
- Strategic alignment.
- Strength of evidence.
- Technical feasibility.
- Delivery effort.
- UX implications.
- Accessibility.
- Security.
- Growth potential.
- Pricing and monetisation.
- Operational complexity.
- Dependencies.
- Risk.

This means a question such as **"Should we build A, B or C?"** can become a cross-functional decision exercise.

Different disciplines can disagree. Product may favour one option, engineering another, growth a third, while research may reveal that the premise behind all three is weak. The value comes from surfacing and resolving those tensions rather than flattening them into generic advice.

## Build features

The repository can move beyond advisory work into execution.

A feature request can be taken through:

> **Request → Requirements → Shaping → Implementation Plan → Build → Verification → Cleanup**

For a request such as **"Add team invitations"**, the team can help:

1. Understand the request.
2. Inspect the existing product and codebase.
3. Confirm requirements.
4. Determine an implementation strategy.
5. Identify dependencies and risks.
6. Break the work into safe implementation chunks.
7. Implement each chunk.
8. Test against the acceptance criteria.
9. Run broader verification.
10. Identify regressions or drift.
11. Produce a release-ready result.

This provides a mechanism for keeping product intent intact as work moves into autonomous coding.

## Red-team a product bet

The team can also be deliberately asked to challenge an idea before significant investment is made.

Different perspectives can attack different assumptions:

- Research challenges the evidence.
- Strategy challenges differentiation.
- Data challenges scale and measurement.
- Engineering challenges feasibility.
- Security challenges risk.
- Design challenges usability.
- Accessibility challenges exclusion.
- Growth challenges acquisition assumptions.
- Pricing challenges monetisation.
- Customer perspectives challenge adoption and retention.
- Adversarial research challenges the reasoning itself.

Product can then synthesise the outcome around a simple question:

> **What survived?**

This creates a useful pre-investment review for new products, features and strategic bets.

The largest combined capability of the repository is its ability to approximate a cross-functional product organisation for a founder, designer, developer or small team that does not have every discipline available internally.

A user can progressively move through questions such as:

> **Is this worth pursuing?**  
> **Who needs it?**  
> **What evidence exists?**  
> **What is the opportunity?**  
> **What should our strategy be?**  
> **What should we build?**  
> **How should it work?**  
> **Can we build it?**  
> **Is it secure?**  
> **Is it accessible?**  
> **How should we build it?**  
> **Can you build it?**  
> **Did we build it correctly?**  
> **Should we ship it?**  
> **How should we launch it?**  
> **Is anyone using it?**  
> **Is it working?**  
> **What should we do next?**

The output of one stage can become the evidence and context for the next, creating a continuous product-development loop rather than a collection of disconnected AI interactions.

## The product-team capability

Taken together, the repository can be thought of as:

> **An AI product-team operating layer capable of investigating opportunities, developing product strategy, designing and validating experiences, shaping and implementing software, assuring quality, supporting go-to-market, measuring outcomes and feeding evidence back into the next product decision.**

Its greatest value is not the number of specialist skills available. It is the ability to combine those perspectives around real product work.

The result is a system that can support the continuous loop of:

> **Discover → Define → Develop → Decide → Build → Deliver and learn → Repeat**

The loop genuinely closes. A health check at the end of Diamond 3 produces the prioritised list that becomes the next Discover, and the assumptions settled there are the ones the next Define will not have to argue about. That loop is the whole reason for keeping a Project Point of Truth at all.
